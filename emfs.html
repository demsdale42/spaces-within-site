What is electro-pollution?

We’ve been talking about electromagnetic fields — EMFs — for decades now. But Bobby finds the word electro-pollution more honest — it does what good language should do: it tells you something true about the nature of the thing.

Pollution isn’t just a substance. It’s a substance in the wrong concentration, in the wrong place, interacting with systems that weren’t designed to handle it in that form. Air is essential to life. Air thick with particulate matter is a different proposition. Water sustains everything. Water carrying industrial runoff is something else entirely.

Electro-pollution works the same way. Electromagnetic fields are part of the natural world — the earth generates them, our bodies generate them, light is electromagnetic radiation. What’s new isn’t the phenomenon. What’s new is the density, the relentlessness, and the particular frequencies we’ve introduced into the environment in the last hundred years, and especially the last thirty.

That distinction matters. Because if the problem were electromagnetic fields themselves, we’d be in trouble far beyond anything technology created. But if the problem is character — the quality and coherence of what we’re living within — then that’s a problem we can actually work with.

Where does it come from?

The short answer is: everywhere, and increasingly so. Before you’ve had your first cup of coffee, you’ve already moved through half a dozen fields — the router, the phone charging by the bed, the smart meter cycling on the other side of the wall. By the time the day is properly underway, the signals layering through a typical home are nearly impossible to map.

And it doesn’t stop at the front door. An electric vehicle carries its own field from its battery pack and drive motor; a gas or diesel car does too, from its alternator and starter wiring — different equipment, same basic fact. Unlike a router or a smart meter, you can’t just move the battery pack further away — it’s built into the car you’re sitting in, which is why a vehicle gets assessed where it actually lives: a driveway visit, using the same measure-and-place approach as a home. Add a home EV charger and you’ve added a new, sustained source to your electrical panel, often running all night. Add solar and the panels themselves are passive, but the inverter converting that power for household use is a documented source of what’s called “dirty electricity” — high-frequency noise riding on the same wiring as everything else in the house. None of that is a reason not to drive an EV or go solar. It’s just one more reason the map keeps changing faster than most people are tracking it.

None of this is meant to alarm — it’s meant to be honest about the landscape before talking about how to navigate it. If a strategy for dealing with electro-pollution depends on eliminating exposure, the world as it’s currently built is going to defeat that strategy. Not because the concern isn’t valid — it is — but because the environment has changed faster than avoidance can keep up with.

Understanding where it comes from is useful. Understanding what it does — and what to do in response — is more useful still.

They amplify. They interfere. They create an environment that no single source could produce on its own.

What does the science say?

A small, unscientific data point: one of Bobby’s own cats has a favorite napping spot behind the TV, right in front of the router and cords. Cats seem to be drawn to geopathic stress and high-EMF zones rather than avoid them — for whatever that’s worth.

Here’s the straightforward part: the science is genuinely unsettled, and that’s worth saying plainly rather than papering over.

The WHO’s International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies radiofrequency electromagnetic fields as a Group 2B possible carcinogen. That sounds alarming until you learn that Group 2B is the category for things where evidence of harm exists but isn’t yet conclusive — a category that includes, among other things, coffee and aloe vera extract. The classification reflects honest uncertainty, not a verdict.

What about safety limits?

One question worth asking is: what are the official safety limits actually protecting against? Most national governments and telecommunications regulators base their exposure standards on guidelines from ICNIRP — the International Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation Protection. ICNIRP actually publishes two separate sets of limits, protecting against two different things. For radio-frequency sources — cell signals, WiFi, Bluetooth — the guideline is thermal: the point at which radiation heats biological tissue. For everything lower-frequency — household wiring, appliances, a router’s non-RF components, smart meters — the guideline instead protects against nerve and muscle stimulation from induced electrical current. Heat isn’t part of that second calculation at all.

Neither guideline says anything about chronic, low-level exposure over months or years — that’s not what either one was built to measure. That’s the gap Building Biology Institute guidelines are built to address: developed independently, grounded in the precautionary principle, and set at thresholds several orders of magnitude lower than ICNIRP’s. Bobby finds those numbers useful as an educational tool — a concrete way to show a client just how much more intense their home’s energy stress is than a genuinely cautious standard would allow. But BioGeometry® itself doesn’t work by chasing a number down below a threshold. It works on a different axis entirely: the quality and coherence of the energy, not its quantity. Two homes can measure identically on a meter and still feel entirely different to live in — and that’s the part no reading captures.

What the research does consistently show — across many studies and many contexts — is that biological systems respond to electromagnetic fields. Exposure isn’t neutral. The body notices. What the research hasn’t resolved is the precise mechanism, the threshold at which response becomes harm, or why some people are profoundly and immediately affected while others seem to move through the same environment without obvious difficulty.

Science doesn’t yet have good tools for measuring the quality of what someone is exposed to — and that’s likely where a significant part of the answer lives.

That last question is particularly interesting, because it points toward something the standard research framework hasn’t fully accounted for. Most research measures exposure by quantity—how much, how often, how close. What it doesn’t measure is quality—the subtle character of the pattern itself. That’s one of the strengths of BioGeometry®: its ability to identify and rebalance that component.

The science isn’t finished. What we can do, right now, is work thoughtfully with what we know — and stay honest about what we don’t.

Electromagnetic hypersensitivity (EHS)

Some people know immediately when something is off. They walk into a room and feel it — a low hum of unease, a headache that wasn’t there before, a fatigue that doesn’t make sense given how well they slept. They leave the environment and it lifts. They’ve learned, often through years of trial and error, to trust that signal.

The medical and scientific community has had a complicated relationship with this experience. The WHO acknowledges electromagnetic hypersensitivity as a real, often distressing experience, while also noting something more specific: in double-blind provocation studies, people who identify as electromagnetically sensitive haven’t been able to reliably tell real EMF exposure apart from sham exposure — meaning controlled research hasn’t been able to confirm EMF itself as the mechanism, even though the symptoms people report are genuine.

That’s worth sitting with honestly rather than explaining away. What it suggests isn’t that nothing is happening — a body doesn’t manufacture fatigue or headaches from nothing. It suggests the cause may be broader than EMF specifically: the nervous system’s own sensitivity to environment as a whole, not just to electromagnetic fields in isolation — the same sensitivity this site talks about elsewhere, where a space carrying its own electromagnetic and geopathic load can keep a body in a low-grade state of vigilance regardless of which part of that load is doing the work.

Whether you identify as electromagnetically sensitive or not, the question worth asking is the same: what is the quality of the environment you’re living and working within, and is it supporting you or quietly working against you? For some, that question is urgent and undeniable. For others, it’s more subtle. But it belongs to everyone.
